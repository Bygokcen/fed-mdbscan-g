\begin{abstract}
Geometric separation of federated model updates can reflect either malicious behavior or legitimate data heterogeneity. We investigate this ambiguity using Fed-MDBSCAN-G, a layered filter combining a geometric-median acceptance region, relative-density gap detection, shared-neighbor cluster validation, and temporal alarm memory. An audited evaluation plans 2,130 method--condition--seed runs across four datasets; 2,125 produce valid results and five retain documented failures. Under attack-free extreme heterogeneity, honest-client rejection ranges from 24.37 to 42.57 percent, and every evaluated round raises an alarm. Across 36 paired ablation endpoints, the full architecture improves final accuracy over its initial geometric filter by only 0.081 percentage points on average. Attack success under the tested patch backdoor exceeds 96 percent. A separate exploratory study compares mini-batch regimes at shared model checkpoints. In a selected MNIST checkpoint, rejection falls from 34 to 18 of 90 honest clients. Matched update-level repetitions show that ten clients avoid additional rejection because shared-neighbor clustering leaves them outside the groups tested for geometric consensus, rather than because they pass that test. These results identify a concrete decision pathway in the local implementation and delimit its interpretation: the checkpoint diagnosis establishes neither general improvement nor security against an adaptive adversary. Filtering, alarm specificity, learning utility, and targeted attack success require separate validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{F}{ederated} learning aggregates locally computed updates while retaining training examples at their originating clients~\cite{mcmahan2017}. This separation limits direct inspection of local training and creates opportunities for poisoned data or manipulated updates~\cite{fang2020,bagdasaryan2020}. A server can examine update geometry, but distance from the majority need not identify malicious intent: clients holding different classes or different numbers of examples can also submit distinct updates.

We study whether a multi-density filter can resolve this ambiguity. MDBSCAN separates density regimes and recovers low-density natural clusters in centralized clustering~\cite{qian2024mdbscan}. Our adaptation, \method{}, combines these ideas with a geometric-median acceptance region and cluster-level consensus checks. The design aims to preserve useful heterogeneous updates while removing harmful deviations. These objectives must be tested independently: a geometric rejection rule does not establish attack attribution, and low attack success is uninformative when the model itself fails to learn.

The evaluation consequently separates four questions. Does filtering improve final accuracy relative to simple aggregation? Does it reject honest clients under attack-free heterogeneity? Does the alarm distinguish attacked from clean rounds? Do additional density and temporal components contribute beyond the initial geometric filter? Matched attack-disabled controls and targeted backdoor measurements address damage and security outcomes separately.

The contribution is an auditable empirical analysis of an implemented layered defense. It links architecture-level security outcomes to a reproducible client-decision pathway: membership in a recovered cluster determines whether an update undergoes additional consensus rejection. We report 2,130 planned units, including failures, rather than restricting the evidence to completed favorable runs. The evaluation provides counterexamples to broad minority-protection and reliable-alarm interpretations of the architecture. Component ablations show that the average accuracy contribution beyond the initial filter is small in the tested block. A separate reproducibility investigation distinguishes completion under deterministic execution from successful learning. Shared-checkpoint diagnostics additionally trace a selected batch-regime contrast to changes in geometric acceptance and in membership of the clusters subjected to consensus validation. These diagnostics explain decisions of the implemented filter; they do not constitute a new defense or independent confirmation of generality. We make no first-of-its-kind claim and no general superiority or Byzantine-convergence guarantee.

\section{Related Work and Scope}
\subsection{Robust Aggregation and Trust Assumptions}
Statistical robust aggregation includes neighbor-based selection such as Krum~\cite{blanchard2017}, coordinate-wise robust estimators~\cite{yin2018}, and geometric-median aggregation~\cite{pillutla2022rfa}. FLTrust introduces a server-side trusted dataset and similarity-based weighting~\cite{cao2021fltrust}. These approaches impose different information and modeling assumptions. Their theoretical properties cannot be transferred to a composite filter merely because it uses a related distance statistic.

\subsection{Cluster Structure and Security Decisions}
Clustering-based defenses inspect structure among submitted updates. FLAME combines HDBSCAN-based selection with clipping and noise~\cite{nguyen2022flame}; it should not be characterized as an ordinary fixed-radius DBSCAN baseline. MDBSCAN instead addresses multiple density regimes through relative-density partitioning and shared-neighbor clustering~\cite{qian2024mdbscan}. We reuse this motivation without assuming that natural clusters correspond to honest participants. Coordinated attackers may themselves form coherent groups.

The adaptation is not a complete execution of the original MDBSCAN pipeline. In Qian et al.~\cite{qian2024mdbscan}, Algorithm 1 uses nearest-neighbor sets without the additional radius cutoff used here; Algorithm 2 applies DBSCAN to points outside the recovered natural clusters and assigns its noise points to the nearest clusters. Our defense instead adds a $3\varepsilon$ cutoff and a minimum natural-cluster size of two, and replaces that continuation with geometric acceptance and cluster-consensus rejection. These are implementation choices whose effects must be evaluated separately.

Our checkpoint result concerns how the local implementation treats low-density points outside recovered natural clusters. MDBSCAN's clustering objective does not itself prescribe a federated acceptance policy for those points. Likewise, the result is not a reproduction or refutation of FLAME's combined clustering, clipping, and noise mechanism. The experimentally supported distinction is between passing a consensus test and never entering a group to which that test is applied.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Optimization and Evaluation}
Unequal local computation is a separate source of heterogeneity. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020fednova} analyze objective inconsistency under heterogeneous local updates and propose normalized averaging. Our fixed-step intervention controls the number of local optimizer steps while retaining the existing aggregation rule; it is not FedNova and does not test that method. The subsequent batch-regime branches address a different question: how changes in submitted updates affect acceptance at a common model checkpoint.

Poisoning evaluation also depends on adversary strength. Random perturbations, distance-constrained coordinated attacks~\cite{shejwalkar2021}, and targeted backdoors~\cite{bagdasaryan2020} test distinct failure modes. A weak perturbation that barely damages an unprotected mean cannot establish strong defense effectiveness. The present work therefore includes multiple attack families and paired controls, while limiting conclusions to their concrete implementations.

The experiments are centralized simulations with statistical heterogeneity and controlled participation. HAR supplies smartphone activity features, but the evaluation does not measure radio traffic, device energy, asynchronous deployment, or privacy leakage. Access to individual plaintext updates is assumed; secure aggregation compatibility and differential privacy are not established.

\section{System and Threat Model}
At round $r$, the server broadcasts weights $w_r$. An active client trains locally and submits $u_{r,i}=w^{\mathrm{local}}_{r,i}-w_r$. These vectors are multi-step model deltas, not single-batch gradients. For an accepted set $B_r$, the full method uses
\begin{equation}
w_{r+1}=w_r+\frac{1}{|B_r|}\sum_{i\in B_r}u_{r,i}.
\end{equation}
Uniform averaging and example-count-weighted averaging are separate baselines. Calling both simply FedAvg would conceal an experimental difference.

The server is trusted and clients have fixed identities. Attackers manipulate their own training or updates; the experiments do not implement identity creation or a general Sybil adversary. Gray-box random-direction probes do not observe other clients' updates. In contrast, the Min-Max and Min-Sum implementations explicitly access the current benign updates and are labeled omniscient. These knowledge models are not interchangeable. Ground-truth attacker labels are used for evaluation and the explicitly marked oracle block, not supplied to the deployable full filter.

Attack ratios in the manifest refer to the configured client population. The evaluation records actual participating malicious identities per round, because conditional participation changes the realized ratio. Main runs use a fixed round-size policy with configured dropout 0.1 and an always-participate malicious policy. Paired controls retain the corresponding experimental identities and schedules while disabling attacks.

\section{Layered Filtering Method}
\subsection{Geometric Acceptance Region}
Given $n$ submitted updates, the initial layer approximates
\begin{equation}
 m=\arg\min_z\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|u_i-z\|_2
\end{equation}
by Weiszfeld iteration, with at most 100 iterations and tolerance $10^{-5}$. It accepts
\begin{equation}
 B_0=\{i:\|u_i-m\|_2\leq 2.5\,\operatorname{median}_j\|u_j-m\|_2\}.
 \label{eq:l0}
\end{equation}
If the raw set in (\ref{eq:l0}) contains fewer than half the submitted updates, the implementation replaces it with the full participant set; for at most two updates it also accepts all. We use $B_0$ below for this safeguarded set. The radius is a multiple of the median radial distance; it is not a calibrated median-absolute-deviation hypothesis test. Robustness of a location estimator does not imply robustness of this acceptance region followed by an ordinary mean. In particular, the geometric median's breakdown properties do not prove poisoning resistance or honest-client retention for the complete pipeline.

\subsection{Density Gap and Alarm Gate}
With effective neighborhood size $k' = \min(5,n-1)$, the implementation computes
\begin{equation}
\rd(i)=\frac{k'}{\sum_{j\in k'\mathrm{NN}(i)}\|u_i-u_j\|_2}.
\end{equation}
Zero-distance and small-population cases have explicit numerical branches in the frozen implementation. Consecutive gaps in sorted densities define a maximum-to-median gap ratio. A ratio above 10 triggers a density-gap indicator and places a split at the largest gap. Otherwise a tenth-percentile threshold is available when a previous alarm keeps the upper layer active.

The current-round attack gate requires both the gap indicator and at least $\max(2,\lceil0.05n\rceil)$ initial-layer rejections. Both statistics are computed from the same updates: this is corroborating geometric evidence, not statistically independent evidence. The threshold is a heuristic; no calibrated significance level or distribution-free false-alarm bound is asserted.

\subsection{Cluster Validation and Temporal State}
Shared-neighbor clustering groups low-density updates. In the local implementation, each point retains up to $k'$ neighbors within three times the estimated radius. Points sharing at least one retained neighbor form overlapping sets, which are merged. Only resulting sets whose size is at least the larger of two and the mean resulting-set size are designated natural clusters. Low-density points outside these clusters are not subjected to a cluster-consensus rejection; they remain eligible if they pass $B_0$. When a density gap exists, the neighborhood-radius estimate excludes the low-density subset; otherwise it uses the broader population. This conditional estimation does not guarantee that the retained subset is uncontaminated.

For a recovered group $S$, centroid $c_S$, and geometric median $m_{B_0}$ of the initial accepted pool, the group is rejected if
\begin{equation}
 \|c_S-m_{B_0}\|_2>2\,\operatorname{median}_{j\in B_0}\|u_j-m_{B_0}\|_2.
\end{equation}
The ordinary combined decision is $B=B_0\setminus\bigcup_{S\mathrm{\ rejected}}S$. Thus an upper layer cannot recover a client rejected by $B_0$. This set-inclusion property constrains minority recovery as well as attack rejection; it is not a security guarantee.

A three-round memory of gate decisions can sustain the alarm until the clean-streak condition suppresses it. The alarm controls whether the upper filtering layer is active. It is therefore not a side channel independent of filtering. If the combined layer accepts fewer than half the submitted updates, the valve reverts to $B_0$. The initial safeguard ensures that $B_0$ contains at least half the submitted updates. This count guarantee does not identify honest participants or ensure useful minority retention; with attackers present it is not an honest-client FPR guarantee.


\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Full-filter decision for a validated round ($n\geq3$)}\label{alg:filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Updates $U=\{u_i\}_{i=1}^n$, gate history $H$, clean streak $c$
\STATE $B_0\gets$ geometric acceptance set in (\ref{eq:l0})
\IF{$|B_0|<n/2$} \STATE $B_0\gets\{1,\ldots,n\}$ \ENDIF
\STATE Compute relative densities, gap indicator $g$, split $t$
\STATE $q\gets g\land(n-|B_0|\geq\max(2,\lceil0.05n\rceil))$
\STATE $a\gets q\lor(c<3\land\operatorname{any}(H[-3:]))$
\STATE $B\gets B_0$
\IF{$a$}
  \STATE $L\gets\{i:\rd(i)<t\}$; estimate radius $\varepsilon$
  \STATE $\mathcal{S}\gets\mathrm{SNNC}(U_L,k=5,\varepsilon)$
  \STATE $m_0\gets\mathrm{GeoMedian}(U_{B_0})$
  \STATE $R\gets2\max(10^{-10},\operatorname{median}_{j\in B_0}\|u_j-m_0\|_2)$
  \FOR{each natural cluster $S\in\mathcal{S}$}
    \IF{$\|\operatorname{mean}_{i\in S}u_i-m_0\|_2>R$}
      \STATE $B\gets B\setminus S$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
  \IF{$|B|<n/2$} \STATE $B\gets B_0$ \ENDIF
\ENDIF
\STATE Append $q$ to $H$; set $c\gets0$ if $q$, else $c\gets c+1$
\ENSURE Accepted set $B$, alarm $a$, updated temporal state
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter} summarizes the full variant with all layers enabled. The density split and radius follow the definitions above; an empty history contributes no active gate. Numerical validation, populations of at most two updates, and ablation switches remain explicit implementation branches. Crucially, the loop removes only members of rejected natural clusters. A point in $L$ outside every $S\in\mathcal{S}$ stays in $B$ if it passed $B_0$.

\subsection{Execution Contract}
The audit contract records accepted IDs, fallback decisions, identities, and per-round metrics. An invalid empty acceptance decision is governed by the configured fallback policy; main audit-v2 uses an explicitly degraded accept-all policy. Nonfinite updates are not silently reclassified as successful results. These operational branches must be distinguished from the ordinary set-inclusion rule and from successful attack detection. The reference implementation is frozen by source hashes; neither the recovery attempts nor the subsequent CIFAR diagnostics overwrite it.

\subsection{Data, Models, and Training}
The matrix uses MNIST~\cite{lecun1998}, Fashion-MNIST~\cite{xiao2017fmnist}, UCI HAR~\cite{anguita2013har}, and CIFAR-10. MNIST and Fashion use a 784--200--10 MLP. HAR uses a 561--128--64--6 MLP with dropout 0.2. CIFAR uses two convolutional layers with 32 and 64 channels, pooling, and fully connected layers of widths 256 and 10, with dropout 0.25. Main configurations use 100 clients, 30 rounds, seeds 42, 137, and 2024, batch size 32, three local epochs, learning rate 0.01, and optimizer momentum 0.9. Optimizer momentum is distinct from the alarm-memory ablation.

Dirichlet concentration~\cite{hsu2019} varies over 0.5, 0.1, and 0.01 in the base block. Main partitioning uses a minimum-count repair policy with 20 examples per client and records the number moved. Therefore the final partition should not be described as an untouched Dirichlet draw. Preserve-empty and five-local-step blocks examine alternative partition/training policies. The fixed-step control matters because three epochs imply different optimization effort for clients with different sample counts.

The plan contains 149 jobs and 2,130 units, partitioned as in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}. It is not a full factorial across every dataset, attack, and method. Three original datasets share a 17-condition base block, whereas CIFAR has a smaller condition set. Supplementary CSVs and the scenario manifest enumerate the exact coverage.

\subsection{Attacks and Controls}
Loud Gaussian noise uses per-coordinate standard deviation 5. The stealth probe adds a random-direction perturbation whose total norm is 0.005 times the original update norm. The legacy identifier \texttt{adaptive\_gaussian} denotes bounded random-direction noise at relative scale 0.3; it is not an optimized defense-adaptive attack, nor does it preserve the update norm exactly. Label flipping modifies local training labels.

The omniscient Min-Max and Min-Sum probes choose a common update along the negative benign-mean unit direction, with a scalar constrained by the respective benign distance bounds. They test that specified direction, not all possible optimized attack constructions. Patch backdoors use a bottom-right $3\times3$ white patch, target class 0, and configured poisoning fraction 0.2. Attack-disabled controls retain trigger evaluation so that naturally occurring target predictions are visible. Oracle results are diagnostic references rather than deployable methods.

\subsection{Baselines and Ablations}
The main baseline family comprises uniform mean, sample-weighted mean, norm clipping, coordinate median, Krum with configured attack bound 0.3, normalized FLTrust with a 100-example root set, and the HDBSCAN FLAME implementation. These labels identify the concrete local implementations; matching every original paper's training and privacy calibration is not claimed. In particular, the FLAME noise setting is $0.001$, and performance here is not a universal assessment of FLAME. A limited check of normalized FLTrust compares its aggregation rule against a NumPy transcription of the author-demo rule on 18 recorded update matrices and four constructed root vectors, 72 input cases per variant. Relative aggregate discrepancy stays below $2.8\times10^{-7}$; running the same inputs through the local server path rather than a restated formula gives $2.4\times10^{-7}$. This is agreement of the aggregation rule on the inputs tested, not fidelity of the whole training protocol, and the author framework itself was never executed. Two boundary cases do diverge: with a degenerate root update the local filter falls back to a uniform mean and raises no flag, and with every trust score zero it applies its configured accept-all-degraded fallback, where the published rule would apply nothing. Recorded rounds carry neither the root magnitude nor the executed aggregation branch, so 135 of 5490 rounds in which every client was accepted cannot be attributed to one path or the other. Our global update multiplier is one; matching local and root epochs does not guarantee matching optimizer-step counts. The effect of that training-protocol difference remains unmeasured.

Seven ablation variants remove upper filtering, momentum, or the valve, or vary the trust-region multiplier. The \texttt{fed\_g2l\_25} identifier is a geometric-median-distance control, not evidence of reproducing the complete published FedG2L system. Each variant has 36 matched endpoints across three datasets and four conditions. Full method parameters remain fixed within the canonical evaluation.

\subsection{Metrics and Failure Accounting}
The primary utility metric is final-round test accuracy. Honest-client FPR is $FP/(FP+TN)$ and attacker rejection rate is $TP/(TP+FN)$, pooled over participating clients. For normalized FLTrust, the client FPR/TPR fields count honest/attacker updates assigned zero trust weight by the local cosine rule, rather than a separate attack-classification decision. This describes zero contribution in the ordinary weighting path; fallback behavior must be interpreted separately. These fields are not directly interchangeable with explicit rejection decisions from other defenses. Alarm FPR is instead computed over attack-free rounds. A false alarm cannot be assessed by restricting the denominator to attacked rounds. Backdoor ASR uses triggered examples excluding the target class. Balanced accuracy and client-group counts are retained in the supplementary data.

We use matched method and attack-disabled differences where the report establishes the corresponding identities. Descriptive averages weight the specified conditions and seeds equally; they do not estimate a deployment's unknown attack prevalence. Three seeds and correlated conditions do not justify treating every round or client as an independent replicate. No significance claim or legacy Wilcoxon result is carried into this revision. Failed runs remain explicit outcomes, rather than zero-accuracy imputations or silently replaced seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports variant-minus-full final accuracy. The full architecture gains only 0.081 percentage points on average over L0 alone. Condition-specific differences range in both directions, so the mean is not a universal equivalence statement. The no-momentum variant has exactly the same final accuracy as the full method at all 36 endpoints, verified against raw run files. This does not show that temporal alarm trajectories are identical.

Removing the valve improves accuracy by 0.278 points on average in this block, with mixed condition-specific effects. Changing the trust multiplier also has nonmonotonic outcomes. Selecting a preferred variant after observing these results would constitute further development; independent validation would be required for a resulting improved-method claim.

\subsection{Backdoor Success Despite Clean Utility}
Under the tested moderate-heterogeneity patch attack, the full method reaches ASR of 99.993\% on MNIST, 99.930\% on Fashion, and 96.589\% on CIFAR (Table~\ref{tab:backdoor}). Corresponding attack-disabled control ASRs are much lower. High clean test accuracy therefore masks a targeted security failure. Conversely, the weaker clean models in the extreme-heterogeneity block must not be credited as effective defenses merely because their ASR is lower.

\subsection{Numerical Failures and CIFAR Repetition}
The four HAR failures occur for sample-weighted mean in conditions 3.1 (seeds 42 and 2024) and 3.2 (seeds 137 and 2024). Isolated repetitions reproduce numerical failure, with nonfinite parameters or updates observed during local training. Correctly normalized aggregation weights do not by themselves ensure stable local optimization. These cases remain failed outcomes under the evaluated protocol.

The canonical CIFAR FLAME condition 7.1/seed 42 fails in both the original and isolated recovery attempts, while an instrumented diagnostic completes at 10\% accuracy. Two subsequent three-round non-deterministic-profile repetitions already differ at the first client's first update. Their partition and initial-model hashes match. Under a deterministic GPU profile, two short runs and an instrumented short run instead yield matching traces.

Two separate 30-round deterministic-profile repetitions then complete with all 2,730 recorded trace entries matching and final accuracy 9.94\% in both. The profile enables deterministic algorithms and cuDNN determinism and configures the cuBLAS workspace. This supports repeatability for this condition and environment; it does not establish portability, diagnose one specific GPU kernel, or validate every CIFAR condition. These diagnostic runs are not inserted into the canonical five-failure matrix, because the backend profile differs. Completion and predictive robustness remain separate outcomes.


\subsection{Exploratory Checkpoint Diagnostics}
We conducted a separate attack-free study at Dirichlet concentration 0.01 with five local optimizer steps. Nine 30-round reference trajectories cover MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and HAR with the same three seeds. At zero-based rounds 0, 9, 19, and 29, three independent one-round branches start from the same model, participating clients, partition, and defense history. Arm A retains the standard batch-32 loader; B draws a fresh 20-example subset per step, without replacement within a step; C repeats the first subset for all five steps. Branches never feed back into the reference. A/B changes both batch size and sampling regime; B/C changes subsequent example identities and order. Neither isolates only the number of unique examples.

All 108 branches completed. In all 36 checkpoint cells, A reproduces the recorded reference accuracy, accepted IDs, and update norms. The matched contrasts are heterogeneous: at MNIST seed 137, round 0, FPR is 23.33\%, 15.56\%, and 5.56\% for A/B/C. At seed 2024, round 9, it is 37.78\%, 27.78\%, and 20.00\%. Separate arm medians across three seeds conceal these contrasts. The study therefore supports neither universal absence of a batching effect nor universal improvement from repeated batches.

Figure~\ref{fig:checkpoint_retention} displays the corresponding acceptance accounting for the selected forward checkpoint. For the latter checkpoint, three instrumented repetitions reproduce the original input-update matrix hashes and final decisions exactly. A rejects 20 clients at L0 and 14 additionally; C rejects 14 at L0 and four additionally. Of the ten clients avoiding additional rejection, all remain on the low-density side but become singleton groups outside the natural clusters tested for consensus. They do not switch to passing that test. The alarm remains active and no safety fallback occurs in either arm. The supplementary evidence reports cluster membership and neighborhood thresholds. This checkpoint was selected after inspecting the branch results, so it is an explanatory case rather than independent validation.

\subsection{Decision Pathway and Exploratory Detection Signals}
Matched fixed-matrix replays isolate why the implemented filter does not reject the coordinated attackers it faces. Across 72 checkpoint matrices and both neighborhood-cutoff settings, opening the gate on the density indicator alone left the accepted set unchanged in all 144 comparisons. The two attacks resist for different reasons. Under Min-Max the initial layer accepts every update and all updates lie within twice the median consensus radius; the arithmetic centroid of any subset of a Euclidean ball remains inside that ball, so regrouping clusters cannot by itself produce a consensus rejection. Under the patch backdoor the density gate does open, in 17 of 18 attacked checkpoints, and most attacker observations are low-density: 285 of 306 in the processed partitions. With the cutoff enabled 239 of them enter natural clusters, and 30 of the 55 recovered clusters contain attackers. No recovered cluster is rejected by the consensus test. These are fixed-matrix outcomes at recorded model and momentum states; they are not equivalent to training with a modified gate and they carry no attack-success measurement.

Because the recorded geometry does not separate these attackers, we examined two directional signals that the architecture does not currently use. The first compares an update with its neighbors. On the attacked Min-Max checkpoints the mean cosine to the five nearest updates separates attackers perfectly. The implementation copies one poisoned vector to every attacker, yet constraint-valid perturbations that break exact duplication still preserve AUC 1, so the ranking detects coordinated directions without establishing malicious intent. On the patch attack the duplicate indicator is uninformative at every checkpoint and unlabeled direction scores average 0.413 and 0.443, below chance. Synthetic controls show the rule is not specific: eighteen honest clients sharing one direction among seventy-two orthogonal honest clients are all rejected, while one to five attackers on the same orthogonal background all escape.

The second signal assumes a clean server-side root set. Using one hundred held-out examples, verified disjoint from every client pool after minimum-count repair, we recorded at each of 72 checkpoints both the increase in root loss caused by a client update and the angle between that update and the negative root gradient. The loss score is rank-correlated 0.82 with the update norm and ranks attackers below honest clients in every condition; it measures heterogeneity rather than intent. The angular score has pooled rank correlations of $-0.17$ with update norm and $-0.12$ with client sample count; these correlations do not establish independence. In the unrotated Min-Max sweep, median checkpoint AUC is 1.00 on Fashion-MNIST and 0.833 on MNIST, with checkpoint minima of 0.681 and 0.167. When each attacker independently draws a direction at a fixed angle from the inverse-mean direction and the same Min-Max distance constraint is re-solved, median AUC reaches 0.512 and 0.444 at ninety degrees, with no recorded constraint violation. Damage from this rotated construction was not measured. A threshold on within-checkpoint standardized scores, selected to retain all Min-Max attackers in development data, flags 64.69% of honest client-round observations in the corresponding attack-free checkpoints. Applied to attacked patch checkpoints, it flags 61.57% of honest observations and 70.37% of attacker observations. These pooled descriptive rates do not establish statistical equivalence or rule out other operating points.

A separate exploratory training experiment examines the Spearman trend of each client's update norm relative to the round median over 30 rounds. It uses MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, three seeds, uniform FedAvg and the full method, five local steps, $\alpha=0.01$, and 20% attackers. An omniscient Min-Max variant targets the honest median norm or twice that norm, giving 24 completed runs and 720 rounds with no recorded distance-constraint violation. Its formula was corrected after an invalid initial run; this stage is not fully preregistered. For target ratio one, median trend AUC falls from 0.994 to 0.585. Median final-accuracy loss relative to matched clean runs changes from 0.4526 to 0.3376 for FedAvg and from 0.1830 to 0.13525 for the full method. The ratios of these medians are 74.6% and 73.9%; they are not medians of paired damage fractions. Positive-baseline paired fractions range from 2.48% to 94.68%, and one full-method baseline has negative loss, making a retained-damage percentage inappropriate. These results concern this offline scalar score; no online detector or backdoor protection was tested.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The central limitation is a mismatch between geometric structure and adversarial intent. Honest minority updates can lie outside the initial region; later cluster validation cannot recover them because its ordinary acceptance set is a subset of $B_0$. Conversely, a backdoor can retain useful clean-task behavior and evade rejection. Adding temporal memory can prolong an alarm without supplying new evidence of maliciousness.

Accordingly, neither the set-inclusion property nor the use of a robust center establishes an end-to-end security theorem. The gap test needs calibration on attack-free heterogeneous populations before operational alert precision can be claimed. Filter decisions, clean utility, alarm specificity, and targeted ASR require separate evaluation. There is no demonstrated system-level privacy, communication-efficiency, or IoT-energy benefit in this simulation.

The checkpoint diagnosis exposes a dependence on whether a low-density update belongs to a recovered group: an isolated update can remain accepted while a coherent group fails consensus. This behavior has been traced in the local implementation, not established for an entire family of clustering defenses. Changes in the shared-neighbor graph, radius, and initial acceptance pool occur together; their individual causal contributions and any adversarial exploit remain untested. Client sample count, label composition, and minimum-count repair are also not separated. First-mini-batch losses do not establish whole-client optimization difficulty.

Table~\ref{tab:signal_scope} separates the evidence supporting each limitation. Honest rejection, lack of specificity in synthetic coalitions, direction-sensitive root scores, and attenuation of one temporal norm score are different observations. Only the profile-aware temporal intervention measures the resulting training damage; the rotated-direction construction does not. At ninety degrees its added component is orthogonal to the honest mean, while the full update has dot product $\|\mathrm{mean}\|^2$ with that mean. Constraint feasibility alone therefore does not establish preserved attack damage. These results constrain the tested rules and scores, without establishing that all geometric or temporal defenses fail or that new information is necessary for every improvement.

The evidence is limited by three seeds, short training horizons, small models, and uneven dataset coverage. Canonical CIFAR results are descriptive observations under a backend profile with demonstrated repeatability limitations. Baseline fidelity, attack direction choices, data-repair policy, and conditioning on successful outcomes also bound interpretation. An improved filter should be developed on designated conditions and tested on held-out seeds and attack settings; rerunning the same matrix until favorable outcomes occur would not address these limitations.

\section{Conclusion}
\method{} combines geometric filtering, relative-density analysis, group validation, and temporal state. Its audited evaluation reveals substantial false rejection and false alarms under attack-free extreme heterogeneity, a small average accuracy contribution from the upper layers in the ablation block, and high success of the tested patch backdoor. A matched checkpoint diagnosis further shows that avoiding membership in a rejected natural cluster can change honest-client retention without passing a consensus test. This observation motivates validation of both clustered and unclustered updates under attacks and heterogeneity. Matched replays further separate two acceptance pathways: the Min-Max updates are never grouped into a cluster that the consensus test examines, whereas the patch updates do enter recovered clusters and then pass that test. Two directional signals outside the current architecture were also examined. Neighbor agreement tracks coordination rather than intent, since synthetic honest clients that share a direction are rejected as readily. The angle to a clean-root gradient loses its separation once each attacker draws an independent direction under the same distance constraint, although that rotated construction was scored for detectability only and its effect on the attack's damage was not measured. The work supports a bounded empirical account of density-based filtering rather than a general superiority claim. The resulting evidence motivates calibrated alerting, explicit recovery of useful minority updates, and evaluation protocols that preserve numerical failures and separate reproducibility from security effectiveness.
